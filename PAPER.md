## Abstract

We present a two-stage diffusion-GAN that learns three-dimensional categorical microstructures from unregistered two-dimensional sections. A low-resolution (LR) stage generates the 3D arrangement at half the source resolution, and a super-resolution (SR) stage refines it. This document reports the LR stage of one run. The LR denoiser is trained only through 2D critics that score pairs of adjacent diffusion states with a relativistic loss, R1/R2 penalties, and an R3GAN-style burn-in, together with section-free losses that force the three axes of each volume to agree and mirrored diagonal statistics to balance. Because no 3D data exist to validate against, the exported weights are chosen by a section-structure score (two-point correlation, chord lengths, and cluster statistics, normalized by the real data's own sampling noise). On independent real crops, the selected step 8500 scores 4.41 against 4.62 for the last step and 5.71 at the end of burn-in, and its cluster error is 0.15 against 0.58 at step 2500. Generated 64³ volumes match the real interface density within 2.5% (0.1197 vs 0.1227) and percolate in both phases (99.8% and 98.5%), but undershoot the pore fraction (0.342 vs 0.367) and show anisotropic pore tortuosity (2.03 to 2.62 across axes). Supplied planes are applied at inference by diffusion posterior sampling, with no anchor-specific training: a generated-control center plane is reproduced with 93.2 ± 1.8% voxel agreement (55.6% without guidance), a real external section with 89.4 ± 3.8%, and the change rate next to the anchor stays at or below that of ordinary sections. Sixteen nested reference planes recover 91.8% of a known 64³ same-model volume. These are controlled generation results, not 3D reconstruction accuracy.

## 1. Introduction

Three-dimensional connectivity controls many transport and mechanical properties, but 3D scans are harder to obtain than 2D micrographs. SliceGAN [3] showed that a volumetric generator can be learned from 2D images alone by judging its sections with 2D critics. That setting leaves two practical problems. First, section realism is a weak constraint on the volume: a generator can keep every section plausible while choosing an unrealistic 3D arrangement, for example by shearing structure so that only one section plane degrades. Second, adversarial training on a single small image drifts: critics eventually memorize the data, and the last training step is often not the best one.

We address these problems in a two-stage system. An LR diffusion-GAN generates the 3D structure at half the source resolution, and an SR diffusion-GAN refines it to the source resolution. This document describes the LR stage. Its contributions are:

1. a 3D denoising-diffusion GAN [16,17] trained only with 2D critics that score pairs of adjacent diffusion states, using the relativistic pairing loss and burn-in schedule of R3GAN [21];
2. section-free statistical losses that encode what 2D sections cannot reveal: agreement of phase-pair statistics between the three axes of each volume, and balance between mirrored diagonal statistics, which removes the shear failure mode;
3. checkpoint selection by a section-structure score normalized by the sampling noise of the real data, which replaces "use the last step" and is computed from 2D data only; and
4. plane anchors applied at inference by diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) [22] on an unconditional model, so a supplied section steers the whole volume without any anchor-specific training.

Generated volumes are plausible stochastic realizations. No measured 3D volume of the material exists, so none of the results below is a reconstruction accuracy.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Reconstruction from 2D observations

Feature-matching and conditional neural methods reconstruct 3D microstructures from a 2D exemplar [1,2]. SliceGAN removes paired 3D supervision by applying 2D discriminators to sections of a generated volume [3]. Diffusion has since been used for 2D microstructure synthesis and 2D-to-3D dimensional expansion [4,5,7]. Property-conditioned approaches control phase fraction or spatial statistics [8,9], while recent volumetrically supervised methods use fixed or sparse observed slices [10,11,20]. Our setting has only unregistered 2D sections and asks for plausible realizations, not a unique reconstruction.

### 2.2 Adversarial diffusion and GAN training

Denoising diffusion GANs [17] replace the Gaussian reverse step of a DDPM [16] with a conditional GAN so that a few large steps suffice; the critic judges a pair of adjacent noisy states. R3GAN [21] shows that a relativistic pairing loss with zero-centered gradient penalties on both real and generated data (R1 and R2) removes mode dropping and that a burn-in schedule of learning rate, Adam β2, penalty weight, and EMA half-life stabilizes training. We adopt both inside the denoising diffusion GAN.

### 2.3 Conditioning on observed regions

Diffusion inpainting preserves observed image regions while generating their surroundings [12,13]. Diffusion posterior sampling [22] instead steers an unconditional model with the gradient of a measurement error on its clean prediction, so no observation-specific training is needed. Our anchors use DPS with a plane mask as the measurement operator.

### 2.4 Generation beyond the training size

MultiDiffusion coordinates overlapping diffusion paths [6], Patch-DM collages neighboring features [14], and GrainPaint applies diffusion inpainting to large microstructures [15]. Our denoiser is fully convolutional, so LR volumes of other sizes are generated directly; tiled refinement belongs to the SR stage.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Problem definition

Let $\mathcal{D}_a$ contain categorical 2D sections normal to axis $a\in\{0,1,2\}$ (planes xy, xz, yz), with labels in $\{0,\ldots,K-1\}$. The sets need not be registered. We learn samples $X\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}^{D\times H\times W}$ whose sections match the corresponding 2D distributions. At inference, an anchor optionally specifies a full or partial plane, its normal axis, and its coordinate.

### 3.2 Two-stage resolution

Each training crop covers `crop_size` source pixels (128). The LR stage sees it area-downsampled to `lo_res_size` voxels (64), so one LR voxel spans two source pixels; downsampled crops carry phase fractions, not labels. The SR stage learns to refine frozen LR volumes by `sr_scale` (2) back to the source resolution. Splitting the problem this way lets the LR model see a large field of view per voxel budget, so it learns the arrangement of particles, while the SR model only has to add boundary detail consistent with that arrangement.

### 3.3 LR denoiser and diffusion

The forward process is a variance-preserving diffusion on one-hot phases scaled to $[-1,1]$, with ten transitions and the continuous-time schedule $\log\bar\alpha(\tau)=-\tfrac12(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})\tau^2-\beta_{\min}\tau$, $\beta_{\min}=0.1$, $\beta_{\max}=20$. The denoiser $G_\theta(x_{t+1},t,z,c)$ receives the noisy state, the transition index, a fresh 64-dimensional latent $z$, and optional conditions $c$, and predicts phase logits $\ell_\theta$. Its clean prediction $\hat x_0=2\,\mathrm{softmax}(\ell_\theta)-1$ is inserted into the Gaussian posterior $q(x_t\mid x_{t+1},\hat x_0)$ to take one reverse step.

The network is a 3D U-Net with widths 16, 32, 64, 64 (three halvings), one adaptive residual block per level, two middle blocks, additive skips, and per-voxel channel normalization modulated by a 128-channel embedding of time, latent, domain, and an optional phase-fraction condition. The phase-fraction embedding is zero-initialized and dropped for 10% of training samples, so the same network also generates without a requested fraction and supports classifier-free guidance. All samples below are generated without a phase-fraction condition.

### 3.4 Pair critics on 2D sections

Training draws one generated volume per step: the current EMA-free model is run from noise to a random transition $t$, and the last reverse step produces the pair $(x_t,x_{t+1})$. Sixteen sections per axis are cut from both states at the same coordinates. A 2D critic receives the two sections concatenated, $D(x_t^{(s)},x_{t+1}^{(s)},t)$, as in denoising diffusion GANs; real pairs are made by noising real crops to $t+1$ and taking one forward step. Because the data are isotropic, one critic serves all three planes. It scores an area pyramid of the section (64, 32, 16 pixels), and the losses below are averaged over levels. Every real and generated pair receives a random element of the in-plane dihedral group (flips and 90° rotations), which are true symmetries of this material.

The critic loss is the relativistic pairing loss [21]

$$
\mathcal{L}_D=\mathbb{E}\big[f(D(\tilde x)-D(x))\big],\qquad f(u)=\log(1+e^{u}),
$$

with each generated pair $\tilde x$ matched to a real pair $x$, plus zero-centered gradient penalties $\tfrac{\gamma_1}{2}\lVert\nabla_{x_t} D\rVert^2$ on real and $\tfrac{\gamma_2}{2}\lVert\nabla_{\tilde x_t}D\rVert^2$ on generated inputs (with respect to the $x_t$ section), applied lazily every 8 critic updates. The generator minimizes the reversed loss. Scoring pairs rather than single samples removes the optimum in which the generator reproduces one of several particle orientations present in the data.

### 3.5 Statistical losses on the clean prediction

Critics judge sections; three additional terms act on the predicted clean volume $p=\tfrac12(\hat x_0+1)$, which holds phase probabilities.

**Phase fraction.** A KL divergence between the volume's mean phase fractions and those of a real crop, weight 1.

**Real transitions.** For gaps $g=1,\ldots,8$ along each in-plane direction, the joint distribution of phase pairs $(p(u),p(u+g))$ in generated sections is matched in total variation to that of real crops. The term is applied at transition 0 only and ramped in over 5000 steps, weight 0.1. It gives the generator a direct, low-variance signal on correlation length that the critic provides only noisily.

**Axis agreement.** In an isotropic material, the phase-pair statistics at gap $g$ along axes 0, 1, and 2 of each volume must agree. The loss penalizes per-volume differences between axes beyond a tolerance of 0.01, for $g\le 8$, at transition 0 only, weight 0.1, ramped over 5000 steps. Unlike the critic, it compares directions inside one volume, so an anisotropic volume cannot pass by being realistic plane by plane.

**Mirror balance.** Axis-aligned statistics cannot see a shear such as $f(y,x-z)$: it keeps xy and yz sections realistic and turns only xz sections into stripes, and in a one-image setting the critic may accept a mild version of it. For each coordinate plane and offsets $(a,b)\in g\cdot\{(1,1),(1,2),(2,1)\}$, $g\le4$, the loss compares the phase-pair table at $(+a,+b)$ with the mirrored offset $(+a,-b)$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mirror}}=\mathbb{E}_{\text{plane},(a,b)}\Big[\max\big(0,\;\tfrac12\lVert P_{+a,+b}-P_{+a,-b}\rVert_1-\epsilon\big)\Big],\qquad\epsilon=0.02,
$$

with weight 10 on every transition. All tables come from one FFT cross-correlation of the probability volume.

The generator objective is

$$
\mathcal{L}_G=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vf}}+0.1\,r(s)\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{trans}}+0.1\,r(s)\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{axis}}+10\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mirror}},
$$

with $r(s)$ the 5000-step linear ramp.

### 3.6 Burn-in schedule

Following R3GAN [21], a cosine curve over the first 5000 of 10,000 steps moves the learning rates from $1.6\times10^{-4}$ (generator) and $10^{-4}$ (critic) to a quarter of those values, Adam β2 from 0.9 to 0.99 (β1 = 0.5), the R1/R2 weights from $\gamma_1=\gamma_2=0.5$ to 0.3 of that, and the EMA half-life from zero to that of decay 0.999. In this run the end penalty scale is 0.3 rather than R3GAN's 0.1 because, with one training image, the critic memorized the data when regularization fell further.

### 3.7 Checkpoint selection from 2D statistics

Every 500 steps, the EMA generator produces eight unconditional volumes from fixed seeds. From each axis, 128 sections evenly spaced over the volumes are compared with 64 fixed real crops by three statistics: mean absolute error of the two-point correlation (lags 0–16, both directions), total variation between chord-length distributions, and the relative error of cluster density and mean cluster area per phase (4-connected components). Each error is divided by the same error between two halves of the real crops, so 1 means "as far from the data as the data is from itself", and the three normalized errors are averaged over statistics and planes. The step with the lowest score becomes `generator.pt`. Cluster statistics are included because merged pores and fragmented solid can match both two-point and chord statistics while changing 3D connectivity.

### 3.8 Plane anchors by posterior sampling

The LR denoiser is trained without any anchor condition. At inference, an anchor with values $y$ on mask $M$ steers each reverse step by DPS [22]:

$$
x_t\leftarrow x_t-\frac{s}{\mathrm{RMS}}\,\nabla_{x_{t+1}}\big\lVert M\odot(\hat x_0(x_{t+1})-y)\big\rVert^2,\qquad \mathrm{RMS}=\sqrt{\lVert M\odot(\hat x_0-y)\rVert^2/\lvert M\rvert},
$$

with strength $s=1$. Dividing by the RMS anchor error rather than its norm keeps the per-voxel step independent of how much anchor area a volume holds. Because the gradient flows through the whole denoiser, it moves the entire noisy state, not only the anchored voxels, so neighboring sections are pulled toward a structure compatible with the anchor instead of meeting it at a seam. Nothing is clamped or pasted: the anchored plane itself is generated.

## 4. Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Data

The training data consist of one 226 × 690 binary phase map. Phase 0 is treated as pore and phase 1 as solid. Material provenance, acquisition conditions, physical pixel size, and an external data license are unavailable, so results are reported in voxels. Random 128 × 128 crops, downsampled to 64 × 64 LR fractions, supervise all three axes under an isotropy assumption.

### 4.2 Training

The LR stage ran for 10,000 steps on an Apple M-series Mac mini (MPS) in 90 minutes, with the denoiser in fp16 under a gradient scaler and the critic in fp32. Each step generates one 64³ volume and compares 16 sections per axis with 8 real crops per axis. The critic has widths 32, 64, 128, 256. The full resolved configuration is saved as `run/20261027-1522-default-lr/train.yaml`, and `python run_train.py default --show` prints it from the current presets.

### 4.3 Evaluation

All evaluation uses the EMA weights. Sampling uses an eight-voxel margin that is generated and then cropped, and classifier-free guidance is inactive because no phase fraction is requested.

**Structure.** For the selected step and the checkpoints at 2500, 5000, 7500, and 10,000 steps, 16 unconditional 64³ volumes are generated with seeds 0–15. They are compared with 128 real LR crops drawn with seed 1, independent of the seed-0 crops used by checkpoint selection, so the selected step is not scored on the data that chose it. Section errors (Section 3.7) are computed per axis from 128 sections and averaged over axes. The structure score divides each error by the error between the two halves of these 128 real crops (two-point 0.0050, chord 0.0218, cluster 0.0355). For the selected step we also report phase-0 percolation and phase-1 percolation (fraction of the phase in 6-connected components spanning the axis), TauFactor phase-0 tortuosity along each axis [18], and the tilt balance of solid particles, defined as the fraction of particles with least-squares slope above 0.05 minus the fraction below −0.05 in each coordinate plane.

**Anchors.** Anchored samples use the selected step and anchor strength 1. Each anchored sample is compared with a same-noise baseline: the identical seed without guidance. *Generated-control internal anchors* take the center plane (index 32) of an unconditional reference volume (seed 1000 + s) along each axis and anchor it in sample seed s, for s = 0–7 (24 cases). Because the reference is known in 3D, agreement with it can be measured at every distance from the plane. *External anchors* take the 128² crop of the training image at (left, top) = (281, 58), downsampled to 64², and place it at the center or at the boundary (index 0) of axis 0, for seeds 0–7. *Controlled recovery* anchors the first $n\in\{0,1,2,4,8,16,32,64\}$ axis-0 planes of a reference volume, in a nested farthest-point order so every larger set contains the smaller ones, for four reference/sample seed pairs.

**Anchor metrics.** *Exact* is voxel agreement on the anchored plane. *Pool4* is one minus the mean total variation between 4 × 4-cell phase fractions of the generated and supplied plane, which tolerates boundary shifts. *First change* divides the label-change rate between the anchored plane and its neighbors by the ordinary adjacent-section change rate of the same volume; above 1 indicates a seam. *Flicker* is the 95th percentile (p95) or maximum of the absolute second difference of adjacent-section change rates, divided by the same quantity in the baseline. Tables report mean ± sample standard deviation over cases.

## 5. LR Results

### 5.1 Training dynamics and checkpoint selection

Figure 2 shows the selection score during training. It falls steeply during burn-in (gray) and reaches its minimum of 2.59 at step 8500, after which it rises to 2.71 at step 10,000. The components move in different directions: chord error settles early, cluster error falls by a factor of five between steps 2000 and 8500, and two-point error *rises* after step 3000. The critic logits explain the late rise. After burn-in, generated pairs settle near −3.4 while real pairs climb from about −1.5 to −0.8, the signature of a critic that has begun to memorize the one training image rather than learning a boundary it can transfer to generated samples.

The anchor does not create a seam. The change rate between the anchored plane and its neighbors is 0.78 times the ordinary rate for internal anchors and 0.91 for the external one, below the baseline's 1.03 and 0.96 at the same coordinate: the guided neighborhood changes *less* than ordinary sections, because DPS pulls neighbors toward a structure compatible with the anchor. The p95 flicker rises by 24–44% relative to the baseline, so the slice-to-slice change rate varies somewhat more along the volume than without guidance.

Figure 5d shows how far the anchor's 3D information reaches. Agreement with the internal reference falls from 93.2% on the plane to 84.0% one voxel away, 74.3% at two, 65.6% at four, and 60.0% at twelve, approaching the 55% baseline of unrelated volumes. A single plane therefore fixes the structure of roughly two to four neighboring sections, comparable to the particle radius on the LR grid, and leaves the rest of the volume free. Because the gradient moves the whole noisy state, the far field is a different realization from the same-noise baseline (about 40% of labels differ at distance 32): DPS does not leave unconstrained regions untouched.

Anchoring more planes recovers more of a known volume (Figure 5e). Whole-volume agreement rises from 54.1% with no anchors to 59.8% with one, 66.7% with four, 78.7% with eight, and 91.8% with sixteen planes, four voxels apart. Unanchored planes follow closely (90.6% at sixteen), so the gain is not only the anchored planes themselves. Beyond sixteen the curve saturates: 93.3% at 32 and 92.3% with all 64 planes anchored, which equals the per-plane anchor accuracy. Once every plane is constrained, the result is limited by how exactly DPS reproduces each plane, not by missing information.

## 7. Discussion

The LR results support three points. First, a 3D generator trained only through 2D critics can produce volumes whose sections match the data within a few times the data's own sampling noise and whose phases both percolate. Section statistics, however, do not pin down the third dimension: the anisotropic tortuosity and residual yx tilt show what remains after the axis-agreement and mirror-balance losses, and both are invisible to a single section critic.

Second, training on one image does not converge to a fixed point. After burn-in the critic separates real from generated data increasingly well while the generator's sections stop improving on two of three statistics. Selection by section structure turns this into a bounded problem: it chooses a step that is also best on independent crops, using only 2D data. Its cost is that the three statistics can disagree, as the opposing trends of cluster and two-point error show, and the equal weighting of normalized errors is a choice rather than a derived quantity.

Third, posterior sampling makes a separately trained anchor model unnecessary. An unconditional LR model reproduces a supplied plane with about 90% voxel agreement, carries its structure a few voxels into the volume, and introduces no seam. The price is that the whole volume changes: compared with a hard-pasted plane, DPS gives up a fixed baseline far from the anchor in exchange for continuity near it. The saturation at about 92% in the coverage sweep marks where DPS strength, not information, limits agreement. Stronger guidance, or guidance during SR, may raise it.

The SR stage will be judged by whether it adds source-resolution boundary detail without undoing these LR properties; Section 6 will report it.

## 8. Limitations

The study uses one binary image, no held-out specimen, and no measured 3D volume. Generated-control anchors come from the model itself and therefore measure conditioning within its own distribution, and the external anchor is a crop of the training image. Material provenance, physical scale, and acquisition variability are unknown.

All results come from one training run, so run-to-run variation is not measured, and no component (pair critic, burn-in, axis-agreement, mirror balance, selection) is isolated by an ablation. The results describe the integrated system, not the contribution of each part.

Section statistics are computed on the LR grid, where one voxel spans two source pixels and real crops carry fractional phases at boundaries that are broken by random tie-breaking. Sixteen volumes per checkpoint and eight to twenty-four anchor cases characterize variation only approximately, and the coverage sweep uses four seeds. Tortuosity and percolation are measured on 64³ volumes, small relative to the particle size, so they are comparisons between generated volumes rather than estimates of material properties.
